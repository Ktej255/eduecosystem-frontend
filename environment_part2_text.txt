CHAPTER 1
THE AWAKENING
1962 – 1972
The world didn't know it was destroying itself — until it did
CHAPTER STORY:
In 1962, one woman's book about dying birds changed history. That single book sparked a 
movement that led to Earth Day, the Club of Rome, and ultimately the first global environmental  
conference. This decade is the ORIGIN of everything that follows — every treaty, every COP, 
every climate law traces back to this period.
1.1  Silent Spring — Rachel Carson (1962)
WHY (Cause): DDT and other pesticides were being sprayed freely across America to kill 
mosquitoes and agricultural pests. No one questioned the side effects on other species.
WHAT (Effect): Rachel Carson published 'Silent Spring' — documenting how DDT was killing 
birds, contaminating soil, and entering the food chain through biomagnification. The title 
referred to a spring where no birds sang — killed by pesticides.
THEREFORE: Public outrage erupted. For the first time, ordinary citizens demanded 
environmental accountability from governments and corporations. The modern environmental 
movement was born. US banned DDT in 1972. Carson is called the 'Mother of the 
Environmental Movement'.
SCIENCE CONNECTION:  DDT is a Persistent Organic Pollutant (POP) — fat-soluble, non-
biodegradable, biomagnifies up the food chain. Bald Eagle and Peregrine Falcon nearly went 
extinct due to DDT causing eggshell thinning. This directly led to the Stockholm Convention 
(2001) on POPs — Thread 3 connection.
  INDIA'S POSITION / ROLE🇮🇳
India used DDT extensively for malaria control through the National Malaria Eradication 
Programme (1958). Silent Spring's impact reached India too — debate on DDT use vs malaria 
control continues. India was the last country to phase out DDT for agricultural use in 2020 
under Stockholm Convention obligations.
1.2  Cuyahoga River Fire, USA (1969)
WHY (Cause): American industries discharged untreated industrial waste, oil, and chemicals 
directly into rivers throughout the 1950s-60s. No environmental regulations existed.
WHAT (Effect): The Cuyahoga River in Ohio caught fire — not once, but 13 times between 
1868 and 1969. The 1969 fire became a national symbol. Time Magazine ran a cover story with  
the burning river.
THEREFORE: America was shocked. Direct public pressure led to the creation of the US 
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in 1970, the Clean Water Act (1972), and the Clean Air

Act (1970). A burning river became the image that made governments act.
1.3  First Earth Day — April 22, 1970
WHY (Cause): Silent Spring + burning rivers + smog in cities + oil spills (Santa Barbara 1969) 
created a groundswell of public anger with no outlet for political expression.
WHAT (Effect): Senator Gaylord Nelson proposed a national day for the environment. On April  
22, 1970, 20 million Americans — 10% of the US population — took to the streets. The largest 
civic demonstration in US history at that time.
THEREFORE: Politicians could no longer ignore the environment. Within one year of the first 
Earth Day: US EPA created, Clean Air Act passed, National Environmental Policy Act signed. 
Earth Day is now observed in 193 countries on April 22 every year.
UPSC FACT:  April 22 = World Earth Day. This is different from World Environment Day (June 
5) established by the Stockholm Conference 1972. Both are asked in UPSC. Earth Day = 
grassroots movement. World Environment Day = UN official observance.
1.4  Club of Rome — 'Limits to Growth' (1970–1972)
WHY (Cause): Post-World War II industrial boom was accelerating at unprecedented rates. No 
one was asking: what happens when resources run out?
WHAT (Effect): The Club of Rome — a group of scientists, economists, and industrialists — 
used computer modelling to project resource depletion, population growth, and pollution. Their 
1972 report 'Limits to Growth' concluded: if current trends continue, the limits to growth will be 
reached within 100 years.
THEREFORE: This was the first scientific, data-based argument that unlimited growth is 
impossible on a finite planet. It directly inspired the Brundtland Commission's concept of 
Sustainable Development (1987) and the entire framework of Agenda 21.
1.5  Ramsar Convention (1971)
WHY (Cause): Wetlands worldwide were being drained for agriculture, urbanisation, and 
infrastructure. Migratory waterfowl populations were collapsing as their wintering grounds 
disappeared.
WHAT (Effect): The Convention on Wetlands of International Importance Especially as 
Waterfowl Habitat was signed in Ramsar, Iran on February 2, 1971. It was the FIRST modern 
international environmental treaty.
THEREFORE: Wetlands gained legal international protection. The 'Wise Use' principle was 
established — sustainable use compatible with conservation. World Wetlands Day is 
celebrated every February 2.
  INDIA'S POSITION / ROLE🇮🇳
India became a signatory to Ramsar Convention in 1982. India now has 75 Ramsar sites 
(2023) — the highest in Asia. Chilika Lake (Odisha) was India's first Ramsar site. Keoladeo

1.6  Stockholm Conference — UNCHE (1972)
WHY (Cause): All the above events (Silent Spring, Earth Day, Club of Rome, Ramsar) created 
an unstoppable momentum for global action. Individual countries acting alone was insufficient.
WHAT (Effect): The United Nations Conference on the Human Environment was held in 
Stockholm, Sweden in June 1972. 113 nations participated. Indira Gandhi was the only head of  
government to attend. She delivered the famous line: 'Poverty is the greatest polluter.' UNEP 
(United Nations Environment Programme) was created as its direct outcome.
THEREFORE: June 5 became World Environment Day. UNEP became the global 
environmental conscience — headquartered in Nairobi (first major UN body in a developing 
nation). The Stockholm Declaration's 26 principles became the foundation for all future 
environmental law. Everything — UNFCCC, CBD, Paris Agreement — traces back to this 
conference.
  INDIA'S POSITION / ROLE🇮🇳
Indira Gandhi's speech at Stockholm was historic. She argued that environment and 
development cannot be separated — poverty itself is an environmental problem. India enacted 
the Wildlife Protection Act in 1972 — the same year as Stockholm — showing proactive 
response. Project Tiger was launched in 1973.

CHAPTER 2
BUILDING THE ARCHITECTURE
1972 – 1991
The world begins making rules — one treaty at a time
CHAPTER STORY:
Stockholm created awareness but no binding rules. This 20-year period is about building the 
institutional and legal architecture. Wildlife gets protection (CITES 1973). Ozone gets saved 
(Montreal 1987 — the only environmental success story). Development gets redefined 
(Brundtland 1987). Science gets organised (IPCC 1988). By 1992, the world is READY for Rio.
2.1  UNEP Established (1972)
The United Nations Environment Programme was created as the direct outcome of the Stockholm 
Conference. Key facts:
•Headquartered in Nairobi, Kenya — first major UN body in a developing country (symbolically 
important)
•Mandate: Monitor global environment, coordinate international environmental cooperation, 
develop environmental law
•UNEP publishes the Global Environment Outlook (GEO) reports — comprehensive state of the 
world's environment
•UNEP administers several key conventions: Basel, Rotterdam, Stockholm, Minamata, 
Vienna/Montreal
2.2  CITES — Washington Convention (1973)
WHY (Cause): International wildlife trade was decimating species globally. Elephants killed for 
ivory, rhinos for horns, tigers for skins, parrots captured for pet trade — all completely legal and  
unregulated.
WHAT (Effect): The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna 
and Flora was signed in Washington DC in 1973. It regulates trade in 38,000+ species through 
a three-Appendix system.
THEREFORE: Wildlife trade became the most internationally regulated commercial sector for 
wild species. India joined CITES in 1976. Under Wildlife Protection Amendment Act 2022, all 
CITES-listed species are now under Schedule IV of India's WPA.

Puma and Jaguar).
2.3  First World Climate Conference (1979)
WHY (Cause): Scientists had been detecting warming trends in global temperature records 
since the 1950s. Charles Keeling began measuring atmospheric CO₂ at Mauna Loa 
Observatory in 1958 — the famous Keeling Curve showing steady annual increases.
WHAT (Effect): The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) convened the First World 
Climate Conference in Geneva. Scientists formally acknowledged: human activities are 
changing the global climate. The conference called for international cooperation on climate 
research.
THEREFORE: First formal governmental acknowledgement of the climate problem. Directly led  
to the creation of IPCC in 1988. The Keeling Curve's data became the scientific foundation for 
all subsequent climate negotiations.
THE KEELING CURVE — What It Shows
Charles David Keeling began measuring atmospheric CO₂ at Mauna Loa, Hawaii in 1958. The 
data shows:
•Annual oscillation: CO₂ rises each winter (Northern Hemisphere forests lose leaves =  
less absorption) and falls each summer (growing season = more absorption) — the 
'breathing' of the Earth
•Long-term upward trend: from 316 ppm in 1958 to 422+ ppm in 2024 — a 34% 
increase in 66 years
•Pre-industrial level (1750): approximately 280 ppm
•IPCC's 1.5°C target corresponds to approximately 430 ppm — we are already at the 
threshold
The Keeling Curve is the single most important piece of evidence for anthropogenic climate 
change — it shows unambiguously that CO₂ is rising year after year.

2.4  Vienna Convention (1985) & Montreal Protocol (1987)
WHY (Cause): In 1974, scientists Molina and Rowland published research showing CFCs 
(used in refrigerants, aerosols, and foam) were destroying the stratospheric ozone layer. In 
1985, British scientists discovered the Antarctic Ozone Hole — a 40% depletion of ozone over 
Antarctica.
WHAT (Effect): The Vienna Convention (1985) established a framework for protecting the 
ozone layer. The Montreal Protocol (1987) provided the ACTION — specific timelines for 
phasing out CFCs, HCFCs, Halons, and other Ozone Depleting Substances (ODS).
THEREFORE: The most successful international environmental treaty in history. The ozone 
layer is now slowly recovering — projected full recovery by 2065-2070. Montreal Protocol has 
universal ratification — 198 countries (the ONLY international treaty with this). It also averted 
0.5°C of additional global warming by phasing out CFCs.

OZONE HOLE SCIENCE:  The Antarctic ozone hole forms every September-October because 
Polar Stratospheric Clouds (PSCs) form during the dark, cold Antarctic winter. When sunlight 
returns in spring, reactions on PSC surfaces release chlorine radicals from CFCs — these 
destroy ozone catalytically. One chlorine atom destroys up to 100,000 ozone molecules.
2.5  Brundtland Report — 'Our Common Future' (1987)
WHY (Cause): Development and environment were seen as opposing forces — developing 
nations wanted growth; developed nations wanted environmental protection. This North-South 
divide was paralyzing global action.
WHAT (Effect): The World Commission on Environment and Development, chaired by 
Norwegian PM Gro Harlem Brundtland, produced 'Our Common Future'. It gave the world the 
definition of Sustainable Development that changed the global narrative.
THEREFORE: The false choice between development and environment was eliminated. 
Sustainable Development became the organizing principle of global policy. It directly shaped 
Rio 1992 (Agenda 21), MDGs, and SDGs. The three-pillar model (economic, social, 

--- PAGE 12 ---
environmental) became the standard framework.
"Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present
without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own

2.6  Bonn Convention — CMS (1979/1983)
WHY (Cause): Migratory species cross national boundaries during their annual migrations — 
making them completely dependent on international cooperation for survival. No single country 
could protect them alone.
WHAT (Effect): The Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals 
(CMS/Bonn Convention) was signed in Bonn in 1979 and entered into force in 1983. It protects 
migratory species and their habitats.
THEREFORE: Specific CMS agreements cover: Amur Falcon (India-Africa migration), Siberian 
Crane (India-Siberia), Marine Turtles, Sharks, Whales. India hosts important CMS species 
including Amur Falcon (Nagaland), Bar-headed Goose, Demoiselle Crane.
2.7  IPCC Formed (1988)
WHY (Cause): Climate science was scattered across hundreds of research institutions 
worldwide. Governments needed a unified, authoritative scientific voice to inform policy. 
Without it, every country could cherry-pick favorable data.
WHAT (Effect): The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) was established in 
1988 as a joint initiative of the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and UNEP. It does 
NOT conduct original research — it assesses, synthesizes, and communicates existing 
scientific literature.
THEREFORE: The IPCC became the scientific authority for all climate negotiations. Its 
Assessment Reports (AR1 through AR6) set the scientific agenda. AR4 (2007) used the word 
'unequivocal' for the first time. IPCC shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Al Gore in 2007. The 
1.5°C Special Report (2018) set the political target for the Paris Agreement era.

2.8  Basel Convention (1989)
WHY (Cause): Throughout the 1980s, a profitable industry emerged: rich nations paying poor 
nations to accept their hazardous waste — toxic chemicals, industrial sludge, nuclear waste. 
Cheaper than domestic disposal.
WHAT (Effect): The Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of 
Hazardous Wastes was adopted in 1989. It regulates the movement of hazardous waste across  
international borders.
THEREFORE: This completed the 'BRS Framework' (Basel + Rotterdam + Stockholm) — the 
three chemical conventions administered together. India is a signatory. The 2019 Basel 
Amendment added plastic waste — now requires prior informed consent before exporting 
plastic waste to developing nations.

CHAPTER 3
THE RIO MOMENT
1992 — Earth Summit
The single most important environmental meeting in human history
CHAPTER STORY:
Brundtland (1987) set the vision. IPCC (1988) provided the science. Stockholm (1972) created 
the institutions. By 1992, the world was ready to act. Rio was the moment it all came together. 
In two weeks in June 1992, three great rivers of global environmental governance were born —  
UNFCCC (climate), CBD (biodiversity), and UNCCD (land). Everything in modern 
environmental policy flows from these three rivers.
3.1  Rio Earth Summit — UNCED (June 1992)
The United Nations Conference on Environment and Development was held in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil in 
June 1992. It remains the largest gathering of world leaders in history at that time.

3.2  The Three Rio Conventions
River 1: UNFCCC — United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change
WHY (Cause): IPCC's First Assessment Report (1990) confirmed human-induced climate 
change. Governments needed a legal framework to address it.
WHAT (Effect): UNFCCC was opened for signature at Rio in June 1992. It entered into force in  
March 1994. It established the Conference of Parties (COP) as the supreme decision-making 
body — meeting annually.
THEREFORE: The framework for ALL climate action — Kyoto Protocol (1997), Paris 
Agreement (2015), and every COP flows from UNFCCC. 198 Parties. The ultimate objective: 
stabilise GHG concentrations to prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with climate 
system.

CHAPTER 4
KYOTO & THE FIRST BINDING COMMITMENT
1997 – 2007
The world makes its first real promise — and struggles to keep it
4.1  COP 1 — Berlin Mandate (1995)
WHY (Cause): UNFCCC (1994) set the framework but had no binding targets. Developing 
nations (including India, China) argued that Annex I countries should take the first step.
WHAT (Effect): COP 1 in Berlin produced the Berlin Mandate — a decision that Annex I 
countries (developed nations) must negotiate binding emission reduction commitments, while 
developing nations were EXEMPT from new obligations.
THEREFORE: This became the template for Kyoto Protocol. The North-South divide on climate  
was formally institutionalised. India and China protected their development space. Negotiations 
began for a protocol to be ready by COP 3 (1997).
4.2  Kyoto Protocol (COP 3, 1997)
WHY (Cause): Berlin Mandate gave 2 years to negotiate. The science was clear (IPCC AR2, 
1995). Public pressure was building. COP 3 was held in Kyoto, Japan in December 1997.
WHAT (Effect): The Kyoto Protocol was the FIRST legally binding international agreement to 
reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Annex I countries committed to reducing their collective 
GHG emissions by 5.2% below 1990 levels by 2008-2012 (first commitment period). 
Developing nations (India, China) had NO binding targets.
THEREFORE: Climate change became legally actionable. Three market mechanisms were 
created — CDM, JI, ET — to make emission reductions cost-effective. India became the 
world's LARGEST host of CDM projects, earning billions in carbon credits.
Kyoto Mechanism How It Works & India Relevance
CDM — Clean Development  
MechanismDeveloped nations fund emission-reducing projects in developing 
nations and earn Certified Emission Reductions (CERs) — carbon 
credits. India hosted the MOST CDM projects globally — wind farms, 
solar plants, biomass energy. Earned India billions in carbon revenue. 
Replaced by Article 6.4 mechanism under Paris Agreement.
JI — Joint ImplementationSimilar to CDM but between two Annex I (developed) countries. One 
Annex I nation funds emission reduction project in another Annex I 
nation. Less relevant to India.
ET — Emissions Trading 
(Cap and Trade)Annex I nations assigned emission quotas (Assigned Amount Units). 
Those that exceed targets can sell surplus credits to those that fall 
short. EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is the largest cap-and-
trade market globally.
Adaptation FundFinanced by 2% levy on CDM project proceeds. Provides adaptation 
finance to most vulnerable developing nations. Managed by Adaptation  
Fund Board. India has accessed Adaptation Fund for coastal and 
agricultural projects.

4.3  US Withdraws from Kyoto (2001)
WHY (Cause): President George W. Bush argued that Kyoto was unfair — China and India 
(major emitters) had no binding targets while the US did. Senate had already voted 95-0 
against any climate treaty that didn't include developing nations.
WHAT (Effect): The US, the world's largest emitter at that time, withdrew from the Kyoto 
Protocol in 2001 — the most significant blow to international climate diplomacy until that point.
THEREFORE: Kyoto was severely weakened but continued. The experience showed the limits 
of a binding, top-down approach that excluded major emitters. This lesson directly shaped the 
BOTTOM-UP approach of the Paris Agreement (2015) — where all countries, including 
developing ones, submit their own targets.
4.4  Johannesburg Summit — WSSD (2002) Rio+10
WHY (Cause): 10 years after Rio — time to review progress. The World Summit on 
Sustainable Development (WSSD) was held in Johannesburg.
WHAT (Effect): The Johannesburg Plan of Implementation (JPOI) was adopted. Key targets: 
halve proportion without safe water/sanitation by 2015; restore fisheries by 2015; reduce 
biodiversity loss by 2010.
THEREFORE: Progress was deeply disappointing. Most Rio commitments had not been met. 
The summit showed that high-level political commitments without accountability mechanisms 
and finance are ineffective. This lesson influenced the design of SDGs (measurable targets, 
review mechanisms).
4.5  Kyoto Protocol Enters Into Force (2005)
WHY (Cause): Kyoto needed ratification by 55 countries representing 55% of Annex I 
emissions. With the US out, Russia's ratification was critical — Russia ratified in November 
2004.
WHAT (Effect): Kyoto Protocol entered into force on February 16, 2005 — 8 years after its 
adoption at COP 3. Carbon markets began operating. CDM projects flooded in from India, 
China, Brazil.
THEREFORE: India became a major beneficiary. Carbon markets created economic incentives  
for clean energy. However, the absence of the US (responsible for 36% of Annex I emissions) 
meant Kyoto covered only limited global emissions.
4.6  IPCC Fourth Assessment & Nobel Prize (2007)
WHY (Cause): Years of CDM projects and clean energy growth had not bent the emissions 
curve. Global CO₂ concentrations kept rising. Politicians needed a new jolt.
WHAT (Effect): IPCC's 4th Assessment Report (AR4) used the word 'unequivocal' for the first 
time: 'Warming of the climate system is unequivocal.' The IPCC shared the Nobel Peace Prize 
with Al Gore in 2007.
THEREFORE: Climate change moved from scientific debate to political mainstream. The Nobel  
Prize made climate change a priority at the highest political level. The Bali Action Plan (COP 
13, 2007) set the stage for Copenhagen — where world leaders committed to negotiate a 
successor to Kyoto.

COPENHAGEN & THE GREAT FAILURE

When the world tried its hardest and still failed — and what rose from the ashes
5.1  The Build-up to Copenhagen
•Bali Action Plan (COP 13, 2007): Set 2-year negotiation roadmap with 4 'Bali Building Blocks' —

•Al Gore's 'An Inconvenient Truth' (2006): Climate change documentary reached mass audiences

•Global financial crisis (2008): Created political headwinds — governments focused on economic

5.2  Copenhagen — COP 15 (2009)
WHY (Cause): The world expected Copenhagen to produce a binding successor to the Kyoto 
Protocol covering ALL major emitters. 119 heads of state attended — unprecedented political 
attention.
WHAT (Effect): Copenhagen produced NO binding treaty. The Copenhagen Accord was a 
political agreement — not legally binding — drafted by the US, China, India, Brazil, and South

African countries felt betrayed. The BASIC bloc (Brazil, South Africa, India, China) emerged as 
a powerful negotiating force. The lesson: a top-down binding approach requiring unanimous 
consensus among 190+ nations is impossible. This led directly to the Paris Agreement's 
BOTTOM-UP NDC approach.

5.6  Durban Platform (COP 17, 2011)
WHY (Cause): Kyoto Protocol's first commitment period was ending in 2012. Copenhagen had 
failed to produce a successor. The entire international climate framework was at risk of

collapse.
WHAT (Effect): COP 17 in Durban, South Africa produced the Durban Platform for Enhanced 
Action. The critical breakthrough: for the first time, ALL countries — including developing 
nations like India and China — agreed to negotiate a new agreement applicable to ALL Parties.
THEREFORE: The word 'applicable to all' ended the Kyoto-era exemption for developing

  INDIA'S POSITION / ROLE🇮🇳
India's Durban position was controversial — India had to balance protecting developing nations'  
right to development while joining the consensus for a universal agreement. India's key 
contribution: inserting 'equity' and 'CBDR' into the Durban text. This gave India the political 
cover to eventually accept Paris Agreement's universal applicability.
5.7  Doha Amendment — Kyoto Phase 2 (COP 18, 2012)
WHY (Cause): Kyoto's first commitment period expired at end of 2012. Without extension, 
there would be a legal gap in climate commitments.
WHAT (Effect): COP 18 in Doha extended Kyoto Protocol to 2020 (second commitment 
period). BUT — only EU, Australia, and a few others signed up. US, Canada, Japan, and 
Russia opted out. The second Kyoto covered only 15% of global emissions.
THEREFORE: Kyoto was effectively dead as a global instrument — covering too little of global 
emissions to matter. This accelerated urgency for Paris Agreement. Simultaneously, Rio+20 
(2012) launched SDG process and the world was building towards a new universal deal.

6.1  Warsaw Mechanism — Loss & Damage (COP 19, 2013)
WHY (Cause): By 2013, climate impacts were no longer hypothetical — Bangladesh floods, 
Typhoon Hainan in Philippines (2013, 6,300 dead), Pacific island nations losing land to rising 
seas. These nations had contributed almost nothing to the problem but were suffering the most.
WHAT (Effect): COP 19 in Warsaw, Poland established the Warsaw International Mechanism 
for Loss and Damage (WIM) — formally recognising that some climate impacts are beyond 
adaptation. A third pillar of climate action was born alongside mitigation and adaptation.
THEREFORE: Loss and Damage gained international legal recognition. This began a 9-year 
journey — Warsaw (2013) → COP 22 (2016, Santiago Network) → COP 27 (2022, Fund 
established) → COP 28 (2023, Fund operationalised) → COP 29 (2024, Fund replenished).

6.2  Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013)
WHY (Cause): Mercury pollution was still killing people 57 years after the Minamata disease 
tragedy in Japan (1956). Mercury from artisanal gold mining, coal power plants, and industrial 
processes was contaminating fish, water, and communities worldwide.
WHAT (Effect): The Minamata Convention on Mercury was adopted in Kumamoto, Japan in 
2013. Named after the Minamata disease. It controls mercury emissions and releases, phases 
out mercury-containing products, and regulates artisanal gold mining.
THEREFORE: India ratified Minamata Convention in 2018. Mercury thermometers, batteries, 
fluorescent lamps, and dental amalgam are being phased out. Coal power plants — a major 
mercury source — must implement mercury controls.

6.3  Lima — NDC Concept Born (COP 20, 2014)
WHY (Cause): Durban (2011) agreed to a universal deal but the format was unclear. How do 
you get all 190+ countries to take action when they are at very different development stages?
WHAT (Effect): COP 20 in Lima, Peru introduced the concept of Nationally Determined 
Contributions (NDCs) — each country would DETERMINE its own contribution to climate action  
based on its national circumstances. The 'intended' NDCs (INDCs) would be submitted before 
Paris.
THEREFORE: The NDC innovation solved the Copenhagen problem. By giving each country 
ownership and flexibility over its targets, the all-or-nothing dynamic of Kyoto was replaced with 
universal participation. India submitted its INDC in October 2015 — committing to 3 major 
targets.
  INDIA'S POSITION / ROLE🇮🇳
India's first INDC/NDC submitted in 2015 had three targets: (1) Reduce emission intensity of 
GDP by 33-35% by 2030 vs 2005; (2) Achieve 40% of electric power from non-fossil sources by  
2030; (3) Create additional carbon sink of 2.5-3 billion tonnes through forests by 2030. India 
updated its NDC in 2022 (Panchamrit — more ambitious targets).
6.4  Paris Agreement — COP 21 (December 2015)
WHY (Cause): All lessons from Kyoto (top-down fails), Copenhagen (binding approach fails), 
and Durban (all must be included) + the NDC innovation from Lima = a new blueprint was 
ready.
WHAT (Effect): The Paris Agreement was adopted at COP 21 in Paris on December 12, 2015.  
196 parties. Central goal: limit global temperature increase to well below 2°C, and pursue 
efforts to limit to 1.5°C. Each country submits NDCs (own targets, nationally determined). 
Ratchet mechanism — NDCs must be reviewed and strengthened every 5 years.
THEREFORE: The new global climate regime. It replaced the Kyoto framework. Its bottom-up, 
universal, ratchet-mechanism approach addressed every failure of the past 20 years. It entered  
into force on November 4, 2016 — fastest entry into force of any major multilateral treaty.

6.5  SDGs Adopted (September 2015)
WHY (Cause): The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000-2015) expired in 2015 — 
they had been narrowly focused on poverty in developing nations and lacked environment and 
equality dimensions.
WHAT (Effect): The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and its 17 Sustainable 
Development Goals were adopted by all 193 UN member states in September 2015 — before 
Paris. Universal application: ALL countries, not just developing ones.
THEREFORE: SDGs are NOT legally binding — voluntary commitments. India is among the 
largest SDG reporters. India's SDG India Index (NITI Aayog) tracks state-wise performance 
annually. SDG 13 (Climate Action), 14 (Life Below Water), 15 (Life on Land) are most 
environment-relevant.
MDGs vs SDGs:  MDGs (2000-2015): 8 goals, only for developing nations, no environmental 
goals, top-down. SDGs (2015-2030): 17 goals, universal (all countries), strong environment 
goals (13, 14, 15), participatory design. Key difference: SDGs apply to DEVELOPED nations 
too — this was the fundamental paradigm shift.

6.6  Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015)
WHY (Cause): The Hyogo Framework for Action (2005-2015) had expired. Disasters were 
becoming more frequent and severe — partly due to climate change. A new global DRR 
framework was needed.
WHAT (Effect): The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015-2030 was adopted in  
Sendai, Japan in March 2015. 4 priorities and 7 global targets — including halving the number 
of affected people by 2030.
THEREFORE: India implemented the National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP) in 2016 
based on Sendai principles. CDRI (Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure) — India's 
global initiative — was launched at UNGA 2019 to operationalise Sendai Framework for 
infrastructure.
  INDIA'S POSITION / ROLE🇮🇳
India is a global leader in disaster risk reduction. India co-launched CDRI (Coalition for Disaster  
Resilient Infrastructure) with UK at COP 26 (2021). CDRI now has 40+ member countries. India  
established NDMA in 2005 (post-Indian Ocean Tsunami 2004). India's disaster mortality has 
reduced significantly — Odisha cyclone deaths fell from 10,000 (1999) to 64 (Fani 2019) due to  
early warning systems.
6.7  Kigali Amendment to Montreal Protocol (2016)
WHY (Cause): CFCs were replaced by HFCs (Hydrofluorocarbons) which don't deplete ozone 
— but HFCs turned out to be extremely potent greenhouse gases (GWP up to 14,800x CO₂). 
AC and refrigeration demand was exploding in developing nations.
WHAT (Effect): The Kigali Amendment adopted at a special Meeting of Parties to the Montreal 
Protocol in Kigali, Rwanda in October 2016 added HFCs to the phase-down schedule. 
Developed nations phase down first; developing nations (including India) have longer timelines.
THEREFORE: The Kigali Amendment is projected to avoid 0.5°C of global warming by end of 
century. It bridges the ozone and climate regimes — and is administered under Montreal 
Protocol (most successful treaty) rather than UNFCCC. India ratified Kigali in 2021.
6.8  Paris Agreement Enters Into Force (November 2016)
The Paris Agreement entered into force on November 4, 2016 — just 30 days after the ratification 
threshold was met (55 countries representing 55% of global emissions). The US-China joint ratification 
on September 3, 2016 provided the decisive push. This is the fastest entry into force of any major 
multilateral environmental agreement — a sign of unprecedented global urgency.

7.1  US Withdraws from Paris — Trump (2017)
WHY (Cause): President Trump argued the Paris Agreement was unfair to the US — placing 
too heavy a burden while exempting China and India. The US coal and fossil fuel industry 
lobbied heavily against Paris.
WHAT (Effect): President Trump announced US withdrawal from Paris Agreement in June 
2017. Formal withdrawal took effect November 4, 2020 — exactly 4 years after Paris entered 
into force.
THEREFORE: Major blow to climate diplomacy. BUT sub-national actors (US states like 
California, cities, corporations) formed 'America's Pledge' to continue Paris commitments. Other  
nations redoubled commitments. EU and China filled leadership vacuum. Biden reversed on 
Day 1 of his presidency (January 20, 2021). Trump withdrew AGAIN in 2025.
7.2  Bonn — COP 23, Fiji Presidency (2017)
WHY (Cause): Small Pacific island nations (Fiji, Marshall Islands, Tuvalu) were experiencing 
existential threat from rising sea levels. They had contributed almost zero to climate change but  
faced losing their entire nations.
WHAT (Effect): COP 23 was held in Bonn, Germany but under the Fiji Presidency — first time 
a Pacific island nation chaired COP. Talanoa Dialogue was launched — a Pacific-inspired 
inclusive dialogue on climate ambition.
THEREFORE: Small island nations found their political voice on the global stage. Loss and 
Damage gained more momentum. The 1.5°C target was championed as essential for Pacific 
island survival — 'We are not drowning, we are fighting' became the rallying cry.
7.3  IPCC Special Report on 1.5°C (2018)
WHY (Cause): The Paris Agreement mentioned 1.5°C but there was no scientific analysis of 
what 1.5°C vs 2°C actually meant for the planet. Policymakers needed this science.
WHAT (Effect): IPCC's Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5°C (SR1.5) was released in 
October 2018. The findings were stark: at 1.5°C, coral reefs decline by 70-90%; at 2°C, over 
99% of coral reefs are lost. Extreme heat events, sea level rise, Arctic ice loss — ALL 
significantly worse at 2°C than 1.5°C.
THEREFORE: The 1.5°C target became the political and scientific benchmark for all 
subsequent negotiations. The report strengthened the case for the Methane Pledge (Glasgow 
2021) and accelerated the call for fossil fuel phaseout. SR1.5 is the most cited IPCC report in 
climate diplomacy.

7.5  IPBES Global Assessment (2019) — 'Nature is in Crisis'

silently — without the political traction of climate negotiations.

THEREFORE: Biodiversity entered mainstream crisis consciousness alongside climate 
change. The assessment directly drove the ambition of the Kunming-Montreal GBF (2022) — 
giving biodiversity its own 'Paris moment.' IPBES is called 'the IPCC for biodiversity.'
7.6  Greta Thunberg & Fridays for Future (2018-2019)
In August 2018, 15-year-old Greta Thunberg began striking from school every Friday outside the 
Swedish parliament demanding climate action. By September 2019, 7.6 million people in 180 countries 
participated in the Global Climate Strike — the largest climate demonstration in history. Youth 
permanently entered climate negotiations — at COP 24 (2018) Greta addressed world leaders with: 
'You are failing us.'

7.7  COVID-19 and the Environment (2020)
WHY (Cause): The global pandemic locked down economies worldwide from March 2020 — 
the largest peacetime disruption to economic activity in history.
WHAT (Effect): CO₂ emissions dropped by approximately 7% in 2020 — the largest single-

8.5  High Seas Treaty — BBNJ Agreement (2023)
WHY (Cause): The high seas — international waters beyond national jurisdiction — cover 60%  
of the Earth's ocean and 50% of Earth's surface. Yet they had NO legally binding protection 
framework. Unregulated deep-sea mining, fishing, and pollution were decimating marine 
biodiversity.
WHAT (Effect): After 20 years of negotiations, the Treaty on Biodiversity Beyond National 
Jurisdiction (BBNJ) — the 'High Seas Treaty' — was agreed in March 2023 at the UN. It 
covers: Marine Protected Areas in international waters; Environmental Impact Assessments for 
high seas activities; Fair sharing of benefits from marine genetic resources; Capacity building 
for developing nations.
THEREFORE: The most significant ocean governance instrument in 40 years (since UNCLOS 
1982). Called the 'Paris Agreement for the Ocean.' Directly implements SDG 14 (Life Below 
Water) and contributes to KM-GBF's 30x30 ocean target.
8.6  IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, 2021-2023)
The IPCC's most comprehensive assessment concluded: without immediate, deep, and sustained 
emission reductions, limiting warming to 1.5°C will be beyond reach by the early 2030s. AR6 provided 
the scientific foundation for the First Global Stocktake at COP 28.
•Human influence is the dominant cause of observed warming since the mid-20th century 
(reconfirmed with even higher confidence than AR5)
•Every region in the world has already experienced changes — no longer a future threat
•Carbon budget for 1.5°C is nearly exhausted — only ~500 GtCO₂ remaining (at 2023 emission 
rates, that is about 12-13 years)
•Adaptation is becoming more urgent and costly — some impacts are already beyond adaptation 
('soft limits')

Is the world on track? — Honest answer: No. But there is still a path.
9.1  Dubai — COP 28 (December 2023)
WHY (Cause): The Paris Agreement mandated a Global Stocktake (GST) every 5 years to 
assess collective progress. The first GST results were expected at COP 28. AR6 confirmed the 
world was NOT on track.
WHAT (Effect): COP 28 in Dubai, UAE (hosted by UAE oil minister Sultan Al-Jaber — 
controversial) delivered the UAE Consensus. Key outcome: 'Transitioning away' from fossil 
fuels in energy systems — historic but weaker than demanded 'phaseout.' First GST showed 
massive ambition gap — current NDCs lead to 2.5-2.7°C, not 1.5°C.
THEREFORE: For the first time in 28 years of COPs, fossil fuels were explicitly called for 
'transition away from' — imperfect but historic. COP 28 also: operationalised L&D Fund (World 
Bank as interim host); launched Health and Climate initiative; established 'Belem Mandate' for 
new NDCs by 2025.

9.2  Baku — COP 29 (November 2024)
WHY (Cause): Paris Agreement's $100 billion/year climate finance goal (due 2020) had largely 
NOT been met. A new, larger goal was urgently needed for the post-2025 era. This was the 
'Finance COP.'
WHAT (Effect): COP 29 in Baku, Azerbaijan adopted the New Collective Quantified Goal 
(NCQG): developed nations to mobilise at least USD 300 billion per year in public climate 
finance by 2035. A broader goal of USD 1.3 trillion/year from all sources (public + private) by 
2035 was set as the overall aspiration. Article 6.4 global carbon market was finalised.
THEREFORE: India, African nations, and small island states called $300 billion 'insultingly low' 
— demanding the full $1.3 trillion as the public finance floor. The Baku-Belém Roadmap 
committed to a pathway from $300 billion to $1.3 trillion. The gap between the two numbers is 
the defining climate justice issue of our time.

9.3  CBD COP 16 — Cali, Colombia (October 2024)
WHY (Cause): Kunming-Montreal GBF (2022) needed implementation plans. A key unresolved

THEREFORE: For the first time, the digital use of biodiversity's genetic information — not just

Indian genetic resources like neem, turmeric, rice varieties have been extensively studied and 
digitised.
9.4  Global Plastics Treaty — INC-5 Failure (November 2024)
WHY (Cause): 11 million tonnes of plastic enter the oceans every year. Projections show this 
could triple by 2040. Plastic pollution is in human blood, mother's breast milk, the deepest 
ocean trenches, and Arctic snow.
WHAT (Effect): The 5th session of the Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee (INC-5) for a 
Global Plastics Treaty met in Busan, South Korea in November 2024. After 2 years of 
negotiations, talks COLLAPSED. Oil-producing nations (Russia, Iran, Saudi Arabia — 'like-
minded countries') blocked any provisions to cap plastic production.
THEREFORE: No global plastics treaty was reached — a major failure. INC-6 will be 
scheduled (venue TBD). The failure shows the same fossil fuel industry that resists climate 
action also resists plastic action — since plastic is made from petrochemicals. This directly 
connects plastics and climate as two sides of the fossil fuel problem.

Extinction Types
Extinction Type Definition and Example
Local ExtinctionSpecies disappears from a particular area but still exists elsewhere. 
Example: Cheetah locally extinct in India since 1952 — still exists in Africa. 
Leopard locally extinct in many Indian forests but globally not extinct.
Ecological ExtinctionSpecies exists in such low numbers that it no longer plays its ecological role  
— functionally extinct. Example: Tiger in many forests — too few to control 
prey populations. Wolves in many European forests.
Biological ExtinctionComplete disappearance of a species — no individuals survive anywhere. 
Example: Dodo (1681), Passenger Pigeon (1914), Cheetah in India (1952), 
Baiji (Yangtze River Dolphin, 2006).
Mass ExtinctionA rapid, widespread extinction event affecting a large percentage of species  
globally — five have occurred in Earth's history. The current one 
(Holocene/Anthropocene) is the 6th mass extinction.
6TH MASS EXTINCTION:  Current extinction rate is 100-1,000 times the natural background 
rate. IPBES 2019 confirmed: 1 million species threatened. This is the 6th Mass Extinction — 
first caused by a single species (Homo sapiens). Previous 5: Ordovician (444 mya), Devonian 
(375 mya), Permian (252 mya — 'Great Dying', 96% of species), Triassic (201 mya), 
Cretaceous (66 mya — dinosaurs).

Greenwashing
Greenwashing refers to the practice by companies or governments of making misleading claims about 
the environmental benefits of a product, service, or policy — appearing more eco-friendly than they 
actually are.
•Examples: Airlines claiming 'carbon neutral flights' through dubious carbon offsets; 
'biodegradable' plastic that takes 400 years; fossil fuel companies advertising 'clean energy' 
solutions while 80% of revenue comes from oil and gas
•EU's Green Claims Directive (2023): Companies must substantiate green claims with third-party 
verification before making them — one of the world's strongest anti-greenwashing rules
•India: SEBI's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) framework requires 
large listed companies to disclose environmental performance — prevents greenwashing in 
financial markets
Direct Air Capture and Storage (DAC+S)
DAC is a technology that mechanically removes CO₂ directly from ambient air (unlike CCS which 
captures emissions at source). The captured CO₂ is then stored permanently underground (the '+S' in 
DAC+S).
•Currently the most expensive carbon removal method — costs $300-1,000 per tonne of CO₂ (vs 
$50-200 for nature-based solutions)
•World's largest DAC plant: 'Mammoth' — opened by Climeworks in Iceland (May 2024); uses 
geothermal energy; captures 36,000 tonnes CO₂/year
•IPCC considers DAC+S essential for meeting 1.5°C — CDR (Carbon Dioxide Removal) must 
supplement emission reductions
•India: Government has limited DAC+S investment — India's primary CDR strategy is forests 
(Green India Mission) and carbon sinks (Panchamrit's 2.5-3 billion tonne carbon sink target)
Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)
CBAM is the EU's policy tool to prevent 'carbon leakage' — the risk that companies move production to 
countries with lower carbon costs, effectively undermining EU climate policy.
•How it works: Importers of carbon-intensive goods (steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, 
electricity, hydrogen) into EU must purchase CBAM certificates corresponding to the carbon price  
they would have paid under EU ETS
•Implementation: Transitional phase (2023-2025) — reporting only; Full implementation from 
January 2026
•India's concern: India exports about $8 billion worth of steel and aluminium to EU annually — 
CBAM will significantly increase costs; India has challenged CBAM at WTO as a trade barrier
•CBAM represents the merging of climate policy and trade policy — 'climate tariff'

Teal Carbon
Teal Carbon refers to carbon stored in tidal freshwater wetlands, including tidal marshes, mangroves at 
freshwater interfaces, and tidal freshwater forested wetlands. It bridges 'Blue Carbon' (coastal marine) 
and 'Green Carbon' (terrestrial forests).
•Blue Carbon = mangroves + seagrasses + salt marshes (saline)
•Teal Carbon = tidal freshwater wetlands (mix of fresh and saline)
•Teal carbon ecosystems are highly productive carbon sinks but poorly mapped and rarely 
included in national carbon accounting
•India's Sundarbans and Krishna-Godavari delta contain significant teal carbon stocks
Carbon Farming
Carbon farming refers to agricultural and land management practices that increase carbon storage in 
soil and vegetation while reducing GHG emissions. It generates 'carbon credits' that farmers can sell.
•Practices: Cover cropping, reduced tillage, agroforestry, improved pasture management, biochar 
application to soil, rotational grazing
•India's relevance: India's Natural Farming initiative (promoted by NITI Aayog) and PM PRANAM 
(Promotion of Alternate Nutrients for Agriculture Management) scheme both align with carbon 
farming principles
•Green Credit Programme (India, 2023): India launched a domestic Green Credit Programme 
where entities (including farmers) can earn Green Credits for verifiable environmental actions
India's Green Credit Programme (2023)
The Green Credit Programme (GCP) was launched by India's Ministry of Environment, Forest and 
Climate Change in October 2023 under the Environment Protection Act 1986.
•Activities that earn Green Credits: Tree plantation, water management, sustainable agriculture, 
waste management, air pollution reduction, mangrove conservation, ecomark label adoption, 
sustainable building and infrastructure
•Credits are traded on a domestic Green Credit trading platform — NOT the same as carbon 
credits
•Green Credit ≠ Carbon Credit: Green Credits are broader (cover all environmental actions), while 
carbon credits specifically measure CO₂ equivalent reductions
•India is simultaneously participating in Article 6.4 international carbon markets AND developing 
domestic Green Credit system — two parallel tracks

Grasshopper Effect — How POPs Travel to Arctic
The Grasshopper Effect (also called Global Distillation) explains how Persistent Organic Pollutants 
(POPs) — though released in tropical and temperate regions — end up concentrated in Arctic and 
Antarctic regions.
•Mechanism: POPs evaporate in warm tropical regions → carried by winds toward poles → 
condense and deposit in colder regions → re-evaporate in summer → condense again → 
repeatedly 'hop' poleward like a grasshopper
•Result: Arctic indigenous communities (Inuit, etc.) have the highest body burden of POPs despite 
producing almost none — eat marine mammals (seals, whales) that bioaccumulate POPs
•This explains why Stockholm Convention on POPs is critical for Arctic biodiversity — local 
emission reductions cannot protect the Arctic without global POPs elimination

Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs)
As Himalayan glaciers melt due to climate change, they form glacial lakes held back by unstable 
moraines (debris dams). When these natural dams fail catastrophically, they release massive floods 
downstream — GLOFs.
•India at risk: 10 Indian states in Himalayan region. South Lhonak Lake (Sikkim) GLOF — October  
2023 — killed 40+ people, destroyed Teesta III hydropower dam
•India has 2,431 glacial lakes — 67 identified as 'potentially dangerous' (NDMA)
•NDMA has GLOF Early Warning Systems — but coverage is incomplete
•GLOF risk increases with global warming — projected 6x increase in GLOF frequency by 2100
•HKH (Hindu Kush Himalaya) glaciers are retreating faster than predicted — 'Third Pole' is 
warming at twice the global average rate
Funga — Fungi Recognition in Conservation
'Funga' is a term coined to give fungi equal recognition alongside 'Flora' and 'Fauna' in biodiversity 
discussions, legislation, and conservation planning.
•Why it matters: Fungi are critically important ecosystem engineers — mycorrhizae support 90% of  
plant species; decomposers; food sources; medicines (penicillin, statins)
•Fungi were historically excluded from Flora and Fauna Acts — making them unprotected
•The 'Flora-Fauna-Funga' initiative (2021) at IUCN World Conservation Congress called for 
integrating fungi into biodiversity policy
•India: Biological Diversity Act 2002 does cover fungi under 'biological resources' but 
implementation is weak
•IUCN Fungal Red List: Over 100,000 fungal species assessed — many are threatened
